What Tark is Link to this section

Tark is a walkie-talkie for phones that are near each other. It connects them directly over Wi-Fi, a hotspot one of them hosts, or Bluetooth, and carries live voice between them. It needs no internet connection, no mobile signal and no account.

It is not a telephone service and not an emergency service. It cannot reach emergency numbers, it does not work beyond radio range, and it must never be the only way you can call for help.

There is no account Link to this section

Tark has no sign-up, no password and no profile. The callsign you type is a label on your own phone, shown to whoever is in the channel with you; it is not registered anywhere and nothing stops two people choosing the same one.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: there is nothing to recover. We cannot restore your settings on a new phone, we cannot tell you who was in a channel, and we cannot look anything up on your behalf — because none of it was ever anywhere but on your device.

  • Harass, threaten or intimidate anyone, or push audio at people who have not chosen to hear it.

There is no moderation and no way for us to intervene: no conversation reaches us, so there is nothing we could review, block or take down even if we wanted to. Enforcement here means walking out of a channel and starting one with a code you did not share.

A channel is not a private line Link to this section

READ THIS ONE

The short code a channel gets keeps groups on one network from hearing each other. It is not a password, it does not encrypt anything, and Tark adds no encryption of its own over the Wi-Fi or Bluetooth link. Anyone with the code, or with the right access to the same network, can hear the channel. Treat it as a radio channel — because that is what it is — and do not use Tark for a conversation that has to stay confidential.

One exception, and it is a narrow one: a guest joining through a browser connects over WebRTC, which browsers encrypt end-to-end as a matter of course. That protects that one link in transit. It does not make the channel itself private, because anyone holding the code can still walk into it.

Riding, driving and your attention Link to this section

Tark was built with riders in mind, which makes this worth saying rather than assuming. Set the app up before you move. Do not look at your phone while riding or driving, and obey whatever your local law says about headsets, earpieces and phones on the road — those rules vary, and they are yours to know.

Anything you listen to takes some of your attention, however hands-free it is. You decide whether it is safe to have a channel open, and you carry the consequences of that decision.

That is the AGPL’s position too, and it is stated here in the same words a court would expect rather than hidden in a licence file.

Concretely, and from experience rather than caution: some phones kill background audio however carefully the app asks them not to; some Wi-Fi networks block the traffic between two clients entirely; some Bluetooth stacks refuse a link that works fine on the next handset over; and a strict NAT can leave a browser guest unable to connect at all.
