What Tark is Link to this section

Tark is a walkie-talkie for phones that are near each other. It connects them directly over Wi-Fi, a hotspot one of them hosts, or Bluetooth, and carries live voice between them. Talking needs no internet connection and no mobile signal. An account is needed only to subscribe.

It is not a telephone service and not an emergency service. It cannot reach emergency numbers, it does not work beyond radio range, and it must never be the only way you can call for help.

Your account Link to this section

An account is optional. You only need one to subscribe; everything free works without it. You can sign up with an email and a password, or with Google sign-in on Android.

Use an email address that is yours and that you can read — verification codes and password resets are sent there — and keep your password to yourself.

The display name and avatar you choose are shown to the people in a channel with you. Names are not checked for uniqueness, so nothing stops two people choosing the same one.

You can delete your account whenever you like: in the app under Profile → Delete account, or by asking us by email. Deleting the account does not cancel a subscription in Bazaar; cancel that in Bazaar too.

Your channels, settings and history live on your phone, not in the account. We cannot restore them on a new phone and we cannot tell anyone who was in a channel — because none of that ever reaches us.

Using Tark responsibly Link to this section

What you say over a channel is yours, and so is the responsibility for it. Do not use Tark to:

  • Break the law that applies to you, or help someone else do it.
  • Harass, threaten or intimidate anyone, or push audio at people who have not chosen to hear it.
  • Record or relay a conversation to someone outside the channel without the consent of the people in it, where your local law requires that consent.
  • Interfere with a network you do not have permission to use, or with anyone else’s use of one you share.
  • Share music or other audio through the app in a way that infringes somebody’s rights in it. What you cast is your call and your responsibility.
  • Choose a display name that pretends to be someone else or is meant to offend.

There is no moderation and no way for us to intervene: no conversation reaches us, so there is nothing we could review, block or take down even if we wanted to. Enforcement here means walking out of a channel and starting one with a code you did not share.

A channel is not a private line Link to this section

READ THIS ONE

The short code a channel gets keeps groups on one network from hearing each other. It is not a password, it does not encrypt anything, and Tark adds no encryption of its own over the Wi-Fi or Bluetooth link. Anyone with the code, or with the right access to the same network, can hear the channel. Treat it as a radio channel — because that is what it is — and do not use Tark for a conversation that has to stay confidential.

One exception, and it is a narrow one: a guest joining through a browser connects over WebRTC, which browsers encrypt end-to-end as a matter of course. That protects that one link in transit. It does not make the channel itself private, because anyone holding the code can still walk into it.

Riding, driving and your attention Link to this section

Tark was built with riders in mind, which makes this worth saying rather than assuming. Set the app up before you move. Do not look at your phone while riding or driving, and obey whatever your local law says about headsets, earpieces and phones on the road — those rules vary, and they are yours to know.

Anything you listen to takes some of your attention, however hands-free it is. You decide whether it is safe to have a channel open, and you carry the consequences of that decision.

Tark is provided as it is, with no warranty of any kind — not of fitness for a particular purpose, not of merchantability, and not of uninterrupted or error-free operation. That is the AGPL’s position too, and it is stated here in the same words a court would expect rather than hidden in a licence file.

Concretely, and from experience rather than caution: some phones kill background audio however carefully the app asks them not to; some Wi-Fi networks block the traffic between two clients entirely; some Bluetooth stacks refuse a link that works fine on the next handset over; and a strict NAT can leave a browser guest unable to connect at all. None of that is something we can guarantee our way out of.

What we do promise is that the app will tell you which part is failing instead of shrugging — that is what the preflight check and the “Something wrong?” button exist for.
