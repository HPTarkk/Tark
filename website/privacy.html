PRIVACY POLICY

A privacy policy for an app with no server.

Tark connects phones straight to each other. There is no account to make, nothing in the middle carrying your voice, and no copy of a conversation anywhere. This page is the whole account of what does leave your phone — a short list — and what never does.

WHAT LEAVES YOUR PHONE

  • Anonymous connection stats, unless you switch them off — which transport you used, whether it connected, and where it broke. Seven events, every value from a fixed list.
  • Your IP address, to the services any web page reaches: the font CDN this site loads from, and a public STUN server if you join a channel from a browser.
  • Whatever you deliberately send us — a diagnostic log attached to a bug report, or an email you write.

WHAT NEVER DOES

  • Your voice. It is played and gone. Nothing records it, and no server of ours is anywhere in the path.
  • Your callsign, the names of people around you, the network you are on, or any address belonging to your phone.
  • Your location. Older versions of Android demand the permission before they will scan for radios; Tark never reads a position out of it.
  • A profile. There is no account, no phone number and no email, so there is nothing to build one out of.

Who this covers Link to this section

Tark (تَرک) is an independent project, not a company. This policy covers three things: the Android app, the browser guest at app.tarkk.ir, and this website.

Every claim on this page is checkable. Tark is open source under the AGPL-3.0, so the code that does — or does not — collect something is public, and you are welcome to read it instead of taking our word for any of this.

Your voice Link to this section

Audio goes from one phone to another over the link you are already on: the Wi-Fi network, a hotspot one of the phones is hosting, or a direct Bluetooth connection. There is no server between them, ours or anyone else’s. Sound is played as it arrives and then discarded — it is never written to storage, never queued for upload, and never buffered anywhere it could be recovered from.

This is a property of how the app is built rather than a promise we are keeping on your behalf. If everyone walks out of range, the conversation does not exist anywhere — including here.

WORTH KNOWING

Tark does not add its own encryption on top of the link. On Wi-Fi your audio inherits whatever the network already protects it with; the Bluetooth link is opened without pairing, so that two phones that have never met can still talk. The short channel code keeps groups apart — it is not a password and it does not lock anything. Treat a Tark channel the way you would treat a radio channel: anyone with the right access to the same network, or the code, can listen. It is the wrong tool for a conversation that has to stay confidential.

What stays on your phone Link to this section

Tark keeps a small amount of state so it can behave sensibly the second time you open it. All of it lives in the app’s own storage on your device. None of it syncs anywhere, and uninstalling the app removes the lot.

Your settings

Language, theme, audio preferences, the callsign you type, and the switches on the Settings screen.

Channel state

Enough about the last channel and the last connection to reconnect you automatically when someone comes back into range.

The diagnostic log

A rotating technical log — session lifecycle, screen on and off, socket events, which peers a packet failed to reach. Never any audio. It is capped in size (8 MB by default, adjustable), it stays on the phone unless you deliberately share it, and Settings → Advanced → Diagnostics has a button that deletes it.

Anonymous connection stats Link to this section

This is the one thing Tark measures, and it exists for a single reason: the connection bugs worth fixing happen on somebody else’s phone, on a network we cannot reach, halfway through a ride. Without a count of which transports fail and where, those bugs get ranked by whoever shouts loudest instead of by how often they actually happen.

IT IS ON UNLESS YOU TURN IT OFF

The switch is in Settings → Privacy, and it starts on. An opt-in version of this gets enabled by roughly nobody, which produces data too thin to act on. We think the honest trade is to leave it on and hold ourselves to a strict limit on what it may contain — no names, no addresses, no free text, no audio, ever — and to say so here rather than bury it. Turning it off does not disable a feature or degrade the app in any way.

The complete list of events

There are seven, and this is all of them. Every value below comes from a fixed list written into the app — there is no free-text field anywhere in this vocabulary for something personal to leak into, and every number is widened into a range before it is sent.

Setup finished

That the intro was completed, whether it was skipped, and which language the app is set to.

Pairing started

Which transport (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, hotspot or browser guest) and which side you were — host or joiner.

Pairing connected

The same two, plus roughly how long it took as one of four ranges — under 5 seconds, 5–15, 15–45, or longer.

Pairing failed

The same two, plus how far it got (permission, discovery, dialling, handshake, hotspot setup) and why it stopped, from a list of eight reasons. This is the event the whole thing exists for.

First transmission

The transport, and whether the first thing sent was speech or shared music.

Session ended

The transport, a length range rather than a duration, how many people were in the channel at its fullest (exact up to three, then a range), how many times you transmitted on the same scale, and whether you left, the link dropped, or something errored.

Feature used

The name of one optional feature, from a list of eleven — self-mute, shared music, QR scan, the home-screen widget, auto-reconnect, noise suppression and so on. Once per session per feature, not once per tap.

Who processes it

AdTrace, an Iranian analytics provider — chosen because it is the one option that is both free and actually reachable from Iranian networks, where Firebase and Sentry are blocked at the endpoint. Their SDK is what receives the seven events above, and it also collects the technical device and install information described in their own privacy policy in order to count one phone as one install.

Their SDK arrives wired for ad attribution, which Tark does not do. The advertising-ID permission it wants, and the checks it makes for whether Facebook and Instagram are installed on your phone, are stripped out of the app when it is built. On an app whose whole promise is that nothing leaves the local link, those would read as exactly the tracking we tell you is not here.

When the switch is off, the SDK is not started at all. It is not started and then silenced — a started SDK has already sent a session ping, which is the thing somebody switching this off was switching off.

The browser guest and every desktop build never load it in the first place; it only exists on Android and iOS.

Permissions, and what each one is for Link to this section

Android names some permissions much more broadly than the use they are being granted for. Here is the whole list, and what Tark actually does with each.

Microphone

To pick up your voice and send it to the channel, live. Nothing is recorded, and the app does not listen while you are not in a channel.

Nearby devices / Bluetooth

To find the other phone and open the link. Only used while you are connecting over Bluetooth or the hotspot bridge.

Location (Android 12 and older)

Older versions of Android refuse to scan for Wi-Fi or Bluetooth at all unless an app holds this. Tark asks for it for that reason alone and never reads a position; on Android 13 and newer it is not requested.

Wi-Fi and network state

To see which network you are on, to bind the channel to it, and to bring up a local hotspot when that is the route being used.

Notifications

So the channel keeps running with the screen off and the phone in a pocket. Android requires a visible notification for that.

Battery optimisation exemption

Optional, and asked for once. Without it some phones kill the channel a few minutes after the screen goes off.

Camera

Only when you scan a channel’s QR code. Nothing is stored and no image leaves the phone.

Internet

Declared because Android treats local network sockets as internet access, and used by the analytics SDK when it is on. A Wi-Fi or Bluetooth channel needs no internet connection and works with none.

Joining from a browser Link to this section

app.tarkk.ir lets somebody join a channel without installing anything. It is a static page: it has no accounts, no database, and no record of who opened it.

The connection itself is WebRTC, which browsers encrypt end-to-end by default — so a browser guest’s audio is in fact better protected in transit than the app-to-app Wi-Fi link. To let two devices that are not on the same network find each other, it asks a public STUN server run by Google what your public address looks like from the outside. That server sees your IP address and nothing else; it is not part of the audio path. Tark runs no relay server, which means audio never passes through any third party — and also means a strict corporate or carrier NAT can leave a browser guest unable to connect at all, with no fallback.

This website Link to this section

tarkk.ir sets no cookies, runs no analytics and carries no trackers or advertising. There is no consent banner because there is nothing to consent to.

It stores exactly one thing in your browser: a key called tark_lang, remembering whether you asked for English or Persian so the site stops sending you to the other one. It never leaves your browser, and clearing your site data removes it.

The page’s font is loaded from a public CDN, which — like any host serving any file to your browser — sees the request and your IP address. Everything else on the page is served from the site itself.

Services we rely on Link to this section

The complete list of third parties any part of Tark touches. Each has its own privacy policy, which governs what it does with what it sees.

AdTrace

Receives the connection stats described above, while that switch is on. Android only.

Google STUN

Sees the IP address of a browser guest while a connection is being set up. Never carries audio.

jsDelivr

Serves this website’s font, and sees the request for it the way any file host would.

ArvanCloud

Hosts the Android download. Sees the download request, as any file host does.

Reymit

Handles donations, only if you choose to make one. That happens entirely on their site; no payment detail ever passes through Tark.

Children Link to this section

Tark is not built for or aimed at children under 13. Because there is no account and no sign-up, the app has no way to tell how old anyone is and collects nothing that would identify a child. If you are a parent and something here concerns you, write to us — but the honest answer is usually that there is nothing held anywhere for us to delete.

What you can control Link to this section

  • Turn connection stats off in Settings → Privacy. Nothing further is sent from that moment, and nothing about the app changes.
  • Delete the diagnostic log at any time in Settings → Advanced → Diagnostics, or shrink the cap on how much of it is kept.
  • Revoke any Android permission in the system settings. The app will tell you plainly what stopped working rather than failing silently.
  • Uninstall, which removes everything the app kept — there is no copy of it anywhere else to ask about.
  • Or build it yourself without analytics compiled in at all. The source is public and the build flag for it is documented in the README.

Changes to this page Link to this section

If what Tark collects ever changes, this page changes with it and the date at the top moves. A change that widens what is collected will be called out in the app’s release notes rather than left for you to spot here.
